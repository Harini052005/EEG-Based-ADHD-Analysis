🎯 The 2 Questions We Answer

🎧 Question 1: Can we use fewer sensors?

Wearing a 19-sensor wet cap with gel can be uncomfortable and overwhelming for a child with ADHD. Can a comfortable headband with only 2 to 5 sensors give accurate results?

🎛️ Question 2: What happens when the child moves?

Children wiggle, blink, and move during EEG recording, creating signal noise. How well do our machine-learning models hold up under controlled noise?

🔬 How the Pipeline Works (Step by Step)

1. Clean the Noise: Filter out slow sweat drift (0.5 Hz) and electrical hum (50 Hz).

2. Cut into 2-Second Slices: Throw out any slice with big blinks or jaw clenches.

3. Measure Waves: Calculate Theta (dreamy), Alpha (calm), and Beta (active) power.

4. Test on Unseen Children: Never let the computer practice on a child it will be tested on.

Clear Answer to Question 1: Yes, Fewer Sensors Work!

When we reduce the equipment from a full 19-sensor cap down to just 2 sensors on the forehead and top of the head (Fz and Cz), we keep 90% of the accuracy while eliminating 89.5% of the uncomfortable wires and gel.

Clear Answer to Question 2: Models Tolerate Normal Kid Noise!

Under mild and normal sensor noise (+20 dB and +10 dB), accuracy stays strong (over 93% retained). The full 19-sensor cap holds up slightly better under heavy wiggling (+5 dB) because it averages signals from all over the head.

📉 How Accuracy Changes as Noise Increases

Controlled Noise Test

🌟 What This Means in Practice

• Mild Noise (+20 dB): Virtually zero loss in accuracy. The computer still reads the child's brain waves clearly.

• Normal Noise (+10 dB): Keeps over 93% of performance. Very dependable for everyday clinical research.

• Heavy Noise (+5 dB & Below): When noise is almost as strong as the brain's tiny microvolts, accuracy drops toward 50% (chance guessing).

🛡️ Honest Scientific Protocol

To make sure our test was fair, all noise was added strictly to the testing sets with fixed random seeds. The models were trained on clean brain signals only, mimicking what happens when a real algorithm meets a restless child in a clinic.
